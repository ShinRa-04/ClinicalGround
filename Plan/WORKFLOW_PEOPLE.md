# People involved in the three healthcare workflows

Public-source research: October 17, 2026. Expands [MEDICAL_WORKFLOW.md](MEDICAL_WORKFLOW.md). The [source ledger](Source/04-workflow-people-sources.md) records 35 selected primary sources, what was inspected, and access limits. R-number citations below refer to that ledger.

Use it for the more precise responsibilities and authority conditions requested after this overview.

**Evidence caution:** this earlier map is synthesis; its substantive narrative/table blocks now have explicit dispositions in the [defined assertion audit](HealthcareRoles/verification/README.md), linked to the detailed role reviews. Composite table review is not independent proof of every possible case. ## Scope and interpretation

The relevant ecosystem spans the patient, provider, insurer, delegated reviewers and community services. There is no fixed universal headcount. A role is a function, not necessarily a separate employee: referral, scheduling and authorization may be combined in one job; a physician may act as treating clinician and later join a peer discussion.

The tables cover **usual functions**, **conditional participants**, and **adjacent/oversight roles**. Those labels are qualitative synthesis, not measured prevalence. Professional authority depends on training, service, organization and applicable requirements. The map is US-focused because that is the benchmark's context.

The official benchmark names provider prior authorization, payer utilization management and care management [R01–R02]. The real-world map is broader. It does not claim every profession below is separately simulated, required for every task, or replaceable by one autonomous agent. Medication, equipment, discharge and external-review pathways are included as conditional context.

| Appointed appeal representative / advocate | Helps pursue a dispute within the appointment | Conditional; not automatically treatment-decision authority | R15–R17, R20 |

**Typical handoff:** clinician recommendation → provider access/authorization work → payer intake/review → provider response → scheduling or another next step. Requests can loop back for information or enter dispute paths. A benchmark task may start midway through the chain.

Noyes describes a specialist following authorization requests through appeals [R04]; Michigan describes combined patient-service, scheduling/referral and authorization work [R05]. These are organizational examples, not a national staffing rule. The expired Mayo page R03 could not be verified in this audit and no longer supports this finding.

## 2. Payer utilization management

This is the reviewing side of the request. A health plan can delegate functions to a medical group or other reviewer. UCSD's provider-affiliated UM example illustrates why employer and function must both be identified [R13–R14].

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| UM intake / authorization coordinator | Receives request, creates case, collects records and routes review | Usual function | R12–R13 |
| Eligibility / benefits specialist | Checks membership/benefit information and administrative coverage questions | Often part of intake | R06, R13 |
| Nurse UM reviewer / appropriate licensed reviewer | Assesses evidence against criteria, seeks clarification and records review | Clinical review; profession varies by service/program | R12–R13 |
| Physician reviewer / medical director / physician adviser | Weighs escalated clinical issues and records appropriate determination | Pathway-dependent review and clinical oversight | R12–R15 |

| Managed-care pharmacist / pharmacy reviewer | Handles medication criteria, evidence and pharmacy-benefit issues | Conditional medication pathway | R10, R13 |
| Delegated-vendor / medical-group reviewer | Performs contracted intake or clinical-review functions | May fill one of the roles above | R13–R14 |
| Determination / notification coordinator | Delivers recorded outcome and relevant notices | Often part of intake/review operations | R13 |
| Member-services representative | Helps patient navigate status, explanation and contact points | As needed | R16, R32 |
| Provider-services representative | Handles operational contact with requesting practice | Common external-facing function | R15, R32 |
| Internal appeal / grievance coordinator | Receives dispute material and routes it appropriately | Conditional dispute | R15–R17 |

| Independent external reviewer / review administrator | Reviews or administers eligible external challenge | Conditional; separate from ordinary internal review | R17 |
| SHIP counselor / consumer-assistance or ombudsman contact | Helps the patient navigate rights or the appropriate complaint/help channel | Conditional Medicare/program-specific support; not clinical reviewer | R35 |
| UM supervisor / manager | Manages queues, urgent work, quality, staffing and escalation | Supervisory; may intervene directly | R14 |
| Patient / representative / treating clinician / provider coordinator | Supplies facts, requests review, discusses case or challenges outcome | External participants, described above | R15–R17, R19–R20 |

**Typical handoff:** intake and administrative checks → clinical review → physician/specialty escalation as needed → determination and notification → appropriate reconsideration or appeal if pursued.

Martin's Point explicitly describes nonclinical information collection, licensed review and physician-adviser escalation [R12]. AZ Priority Care separately describes intake, eligibility, routing and notification work [R13]. These are concrete examples, not a universal national rule about who signs every approval or denial.

R12 is an earlier captured excerpt after a failed fresh retrieval. R13 permits specified limited nonclinical approvals/benefit decisions under delegation, excludes AZPC appeals delegation, and contains unresolved scope tension between wider professional categories and medical-director final responsibility. These qualifications matter before applying a simple intake-to-clinician sequence; see [D11](HealthcareRoles/DECISIONS.md#d11) and U01–U08.

Keep benefit/administrative problems distinct from medical-necessity assessment. A peer discussion, internal appeal and independent external review are different processes. A payer UM nurse and a hospital discharge/UR nurse may have similar credentials while acting for different organizations and purposes.

## Interpretation for this project

1. **Role versus person:** peer-to-peer is an interaction between existing clinicians; coordinator titles can overlap. Table rows are not a headcount.
2. **Side versus title:** identify provider, payer, delegated group and patient/community perspective. The same RN credential can underlie different responsibilities.
3. **Clinical decision versus coordination:** recommending treatment, reviewing coverage, collecting evidence, moving records and expressing patient choices carry different responsibilities.
4. **Real world versus simulation:** benchmark tasks sample this ecosystem. No claim is made that all roles are separately simulated or that one agent replaces all professions.

The closest everyday operational counterparts are a **provider authorization coordinator**, a **payer clinical-review team**, and a **care manager working with the patient and a multidisciplinary team**. This is a functional interpretation, not a formal occupational classification issued by the benchmark.

## Research state and open work

This is desk research and a source-backed synthesis, not an observed staffing census, interview study, legal scope-of-practice determination or clinical validation. Some older sources establish role examples; the ledger marks indexed-only or inaccessible material. No private handbook content was used. No agent architecture, implementation, model trial, provider API call, external message or submission was performed.

A task-by-task mapping to public agent-facing instructions remains unperformed. It could clarify which subset of these people and responsibilities appears in each specific benchmark task.
